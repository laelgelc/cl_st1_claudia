## Factor f5 (negative): embodied engagement, choice, and interpersonal attention

**Recommended label: “Embodied engagement and personal choice”**

This pole provisionally brings together discourse about **responding to music, exercising preferences, and attending to other people**. The excerpts suggest two overlapping strands: participatory musical enjoyment and interpersonal attraction or evaluation. These are not uniformly celebratory: choice can produce freedom and pleasure, but also loneliness, rejection, or exposure to others’ scrutiny.

The interpretation should remain broad and qualified. The eight loading words form a relatively heterogeneous set, and the strongest-loading item, **sunshine**, is not illustrated in the supplied excerpts.

Thus, the pole is not securely reducible to “dance” or “romance.” **Choice, attention, bodily engagement, and affective response** provide a more inclusive interpretation. Equally, the prominent *sunshine* and *mmm* loadings mean that the excerpts do not exhaust the discourse represented across the analysed transcript subcorpus.

### Evidence from the excerpts

**Musical participation and expressive freedom** are especially clear in the *rhythm* examples. “The Loco-Motion” connects “a little bit of **rhythm**” with invitations to swing hips, jump, move around the floor, and dance together. The party-oriented excerpt repeatedly urges listeners to “rock … to the **rhythm**,” alongside “make your body sway,” “socialize,” and “be what you want to be.” This links bodily participation with personal freedom.

The rock-and-roll excerpt supplies a bridge between musical engagement and preference: “Any old way you **choose** it” leads into “If you wanna dance with me.” However, the reggae excerpt complicates a purely recreational interpretation: **rhythm** appears alongside references to guns, alternative solutions, and wealth distribution. Musical vocabulary can therefore participate in socially critical discourse as well as dance invitations.

**Interpersonal choice and attention** form the other substantial strand. “Give your love … to whoever you **choose**” explicitly frames intimacy as personal autonomy. By contrast, “you’ll do just what you **choose** to do” accompanies the speaker’s expectation of being alone. Choice is consequently not always empowering for everyone involved.

The **stare** examples likewise vary. “I can’t stop staring” expresses sexual attraction; “they all stop and **stare**” presents public scrutiny of grief; and staring into another person’s eyes occurs within a narrative of dispossession and social vulnerability. These contexts support **interpersonal attention and evaluation**, rather than attraction alone.

### Decade pattern

The **2010s drive this negative pole**:

- **2010s:** −0.0340  
- **2020s:** +0.0053  
- **2000s:** +0.0223  

The 2010s have the lowest mean, and all supplied illustrative texts belong to that stratum. Nevertheless, the means are close to zero and their differences are small relative to the reported standard deviations. The pattern suggests a **modest concentration in the 2010s**, not a strong or steadily developing diachronic trend.

These are dataset-stratum findings. References to earlier periods within lyrics do not establish additional decades in this analysis or justify claims about the historical emergence of this discourse.

### Alternative labels

- **“Musical participation and interpersonal choice”** — the clearest, most directly grounded label for the supplied excerpts, though less accommodating of *sunshine* and *mmm*.
- **“Affective engagement, preference, and social attention”** — broader coverage of the full loading set, but less specific about embodied musical participation.

Overall, **“Embodied engagement and personal choice”** offers a useful provisional label without treating this heterogeneous pole as a single, uniformly positive discourse or extending transcript evidence into claims about sonic or visual meaning.
